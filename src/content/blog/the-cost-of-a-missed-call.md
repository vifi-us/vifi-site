The cost of a missed business call is not a universal statistic. It depends on why the person called, the value of the job, whether they can wait, and how quickly your team follows up.

That is good news: you can estimate the opportunity using your own numbers instead of relying on an industry-wide claim.

## A simple missed-call cost formula

Start with this monthly estimate:

**Missed calls × average job value × realistic booking rate = estimated monthly opportunity**

For example, if your records show 10 missed calls, your typical job is worth $300, and you believe 20% of qualified callers would book after a timely response:

**10 × $300 × 20% = $600 in estimated monthly opportunity**

Treat that number as a decision aid, not a forecast. Some callers are existing customers, vendors, spam, or people outside your service area. The goal is to learn what your calls are actually worth and whether better coverage justifies the cost.

## Run a five-minute missed-call audit

Pull one month of call history from your phone system and record:

1. **Total inbound calls.** Exclude clearly identified spam if your phone system labels it reliably.
2. **Calls answered by a person.** Count calls your team actually connected with.
3. **Calls handled by another system.** Include an answering service, call center, or AI receptionist.
4. **Calls that reached voicemail or disconnected.** Review the time of day and day of week.
5. **Qualified opportunities.** Sample the missed calls you returned and note how many fit your service area and job types.
6. **Booked work.** Use your own close rate and average completed-job value rather than a generic benchmark.

This audit gives you a defensible booking-rate input for the formula. Repeat it after changing your call workflow so you can compare like with like.

## Find the coverage gap before buying anything

Group missed calls by:

- **Time:** business hours, lunch, evenings, weekends, and on-call periods
- **Cause:** staff already on a call, driving, working on site, closed, or intentionally screening
- **Caller need:** emergency request, estimate, appointment change, existing-customer question, or unrelated call
- **Outcome:** booked, lost, disqualified, unresolved, or unknown

The pattern should determine the response. A few overflow calls during busy periods need a different solution from consistent after-hours demand.

## Compare the practical options

| Approach | Best fit | Tradeoff to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Faster callback process | Low call volume with reliable notifications | The caller still waits and your team starts without context |
| Shared team routing | Several people can answer during business hours | Ownership and handoff rules must be clear |
| Human answering service | Calls need flexible human judgment | Quality, training, availability, and cost vary |
| AI receptionist | Repetitive intake, overflow, or after-hours coverage | Needs clear boundaries, realistic testing, and a human escalation path |

No option should be trusted on a polished demo alone. Use real intake questions, awkward phrasing, out-of-scope requests, urgent situations, and calls that should reach a person.

## A safer way to test an AI receptionist

Start narrow. For a home-service business, that might be after-hours or overflow intake for one service line.

1. Define the facts the assistant may state and the promises it must not make.
2. Write the minimum intake questions your team needs for a useful follow-up.
3. Add an explicit path for emergencies and requests that need a person.
4. Make test calls using realistic customer situations.
5. Review the summary, transcript, recording, and notification speed.
6. Compare answered calls, qualified opportunities, and booked work with your audit baseline.
