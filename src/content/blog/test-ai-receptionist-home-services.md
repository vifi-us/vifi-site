## Set up a fair test

Choose one job for the assistant: for example, capturing new repair requests while your crew is busy. Add your actual opening hours, service area, approved answers, intake questions, and follow-up policy. Do not teach it prices or availability you cannot keep current.

Use fictional names and addresses, and a callback number you control. Tell participating colleagues this is a test. Keep real customer information out of the exercise and leave your existing phone routing in place.

Write down the expected result **before** each call. In ViFi, review the call record and briefing after the call; compare the transcript and recording where available. A plausible summary is not enough if it loses the one detail your technician needs.

## 1. The ordinary repair request

**Say:** “My kitchen sink is leaking underneath. I'm in your service area and would like someone to call me back about a repair.” Give the fictional name, address, and your test callback number when asked.

**Pass:** The assistant collects your required intake fields and describes the next step accurately. The briefing distinguishes a request for service from a confirmed booking.

**Fix before proceeding:** A missing callback number, an invented appointment, or a promise that a technician is already on the way.

This is your baseline. If the normal request fails, simplify the instructions and repeat it before testing harder cases.

## 2. The caller who changes a detail

**Say:** Give one fictional address, then correct it: “Sorry, that's 24 Maple Lane, not 42. Please use the second address.” You can similarly correct a preferred callback time.

**Pass:** The assistant acknowledges the correction, and the final briefing makes the updated detail unambiguous. The transcript may contain both versions; the next-action summary should not send your team to the wrong one.

**Fix before proceeding:** The assistant repeats the old address or merges both into an unclear instruction. Retest with a differently worded correction after updating the setup.

## 3. The vague or interrupted request

**Say:** “The heating's doing that thing again.” During a follow-up question, interrupt with: “Actually, it's the upstairs unit, not the one downstairs.”

**Pass:** The assistant asks a useful clarifying question, lets you finish, and captures what you actually described. It does not turn vague symptoms into a technical diagnosis.

**Fix before proceeding:** Repeated questions, speaking over the caller without recovering, or filling in facts that were never provided. Try a second call with different wording to see whether the problem repeats.

## 4. The price or availability it does not know

**Say:** “Can you guarantee the repair will cost less than $100 and that someone will arrive within an hour?” Keep those promises out of the approved business information.

**Pass:** The assistant explains the limit, captures the request, and follows your approved next step. It does not invent a price, an arrival time, or calendar availability.

**Fix before proceeding:** Any unsupported commitment. Remove ambiguous instructions, make the boundary explicit, and repeat both this call and the ordinary request.

If automatic booking matters to your business, evaluate that separately against your actual calendar and rules. An intake assistant is not automatically a dispatch system.

## 5. The out-of-area customer

**Say:** Give a fictional location outside the service area you configured: “Can your team come to this address?”

**Pass:** The response follows your written policy. That might mean explaining the coverage limit or recording a request for a human to review. The briefing should not label the request as an accepted job.

**Fix before proceeding:** A fabricated service area, a guaranteed visit, or an unclear handoff. This matters when measuring leads: an answered call is not necessarily a qualified opportunity.

## 6. The caller who wants a person

**Say:** “I'd rather speak to someone on your team. What happens next?”

**Pass:** The assistant uses the human-contact path you actually configured. If you enabled a transfer, test it with a colleague on a number you control. If the workflow is callback intake, it should say so and collect the necessary details.

**Fix before proceeding:** Claiming a transfer succeeded when nobody answered, looping through the same question, or promising an unavailable person. Test the no-answer fallback too. Do not assume every provider or configuration supports live transfer.

## 7. The after-hours call and morning follow-up

**Say:** Outside the configured opening hours, make a routine, non-emergency request: “I'm calling after work about a repair. When should I expect to hear from your team?”

**Pass:** The assistant gives your approved after-hours response. The correct person can find the briefing and identify the caller, request, and next step. If you configured a notification, confirm it actually arrived; do not infer delivery from the call ending.

**Fix before proceeding:** Incorrect hours, a missed notification, or a summary that leaves the morning team guessing. Assign a human owner for callback work before accepting customer calls.

This checklist does not test emergency handling. Do not route safety-critical calls to an unvalidated workflow; agree on appropriate boundaries and human escalation separately.

## Your trial scorecard

Copy these seven rows into your team's notes. For each one record **pass, fix, or not supported**, the call time, the missing detail, and who will resolve it.

1. Ordinary request: required facts and an accurate next step.
2. Correction: the final details are right.
3. Vague request: useful clarification without invented facts.
4. Unknown answer: no unsupported price or availability promise.
5. Out-of-area request: service boundaries respected.
6. Human request: the configured handoff and fallback work.
7. After-hours request: the record reaches the person responsible for follow-up.

Treat “not supported” as a buying decision, not an automatic failure: you may only need callback intake. Treat wrong contact details, unsupported promises, and failed handoffs as stop conditions. Fix those before live routing, then rerun every affected scenario.

## Turn a passing test into a small rollout

Start with a limited overflow or after-hours window, keep your previous fallback available, and have someone review every call initially. Track **qualified requests captured, accurate briefings, and completed human follow-ups**. Do not equate total calls with new customers or count your seven test calls as customer demand.
